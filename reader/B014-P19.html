SIX WAYS A GOOD-LOOKING ROUTE FAILS

Use the failure atlas to recognize false confidence before moving.

SCENARIO · A single confident assumption can turn a nearby trail into a dangerous detour.
Options from least equipment
NO GEAR: Do not improvise a heading from moss, wind or stream flow.
IMPROVISED: Use simple notes or signs to describe the last known position, not to invent a track.
EQUIPPED: Use verified map features and a working compass/GPS, cross-checking both.
Four-action sequence
  1. NOTICE — Look for a route assumption rather than a measured fact.
  2. COMPARE — Check terrain, weather, map and navigation tools independently.
  3. CORRECT — Reject unsupported shortcuts and reset to a verified known location.
  4. ABORT — If contradictions remain, shelter and contact help rather than continue.

Check before continuing

  • Do route signs and current terrain agree?
  • Have conditions made an earlier trail or water crossing unsafe?
  • Could one tool, clue or memory be wrong?

Wrong signal → correction

Follow boot tracks → Snow-covered tracks can lead away or vanish.
Cross a white lake → Ice can vary and fail without warning.
Moss means north → Environmental growth is not a bearing.
GPS dot means trail → Coordinates are not passability.
Map omits swamp → Map age/scale and change matter.
Keep walking to search → Random movement increases search area.
STOP / ABORT CONDITION: If the navigation evidence is inconsistent, do not guess. Stop in a safe location and request help.
