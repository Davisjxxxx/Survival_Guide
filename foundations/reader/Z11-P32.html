Z11-P32 · Navigation, weather and rescue · Adapted historical material

Box cleanly around a pond, cliff or blowdown using right-angle offsets

Detour around a compact obstacle on a bearing leg and return approximately to your original travel line on the far side, then confirm your position.

When it applies · Encountering a small beaver pond, blowdown tangle or rock ledge along a verified dry-land compass leg.

Equipment · Baseplate compass, pace count and clear obstacle observation.

Actions

  1. Stop safely short of the obstacle, add or subtract 90 degrees on your compass bezel toward the clearer safer side and count paces walking perpendicular until clear of the barrier.[N08, N10, ED]
  2. Return the bezel to your original travel bearing and walk forward until clear of the obstacle's far end.[N08, ED]
  3. Reverse the 90-degree turn back toward your original line and pace the same distance as your first offset leg, adding paces where slope or brush shortens your stride, then reset to your original bearing; the regained line is approximate.[N08, N15, ED]

Failure recognition · Guessing an angled curve around a pond without counting paces, drifting hundreds of yards off the original bearing and missing the next checkpoint.[N08, N10]

Verify · A matching count only estimates the return; confirm your position against map landforms or a known feature before relying on the resumed line.[N15, N03, ED]

Stop or change course · Use right-angle boxing only for compact obstacles on safe firm land; large lakes, cliffs or extensive wetlands require full map rerouting, not blind pacing.[N10, X12, N02]

N02 · USGS · Topographic Map Symbols (agency PDF)
Section: Topographic Map Symbols > Contours, Water, Vegetation and Boundaries · Conditions: Interpreting standard USGS 1:24,000 and US Topo map sheets
Support: Contour spacing indicates slope steepness, index contours give elevation values, blue features mark water/wetlands and standard symbols distinguish roads, trails, cliffs and marshes.
Limitations: Small terrain hazards (boulders, undercut banks, fallen timber, thin ice) fall below map contour interval resolution. · Conflicting evidence: None; anchors terrain and contour interpretation cards.
Editorial note: Explains contour reading alongside map-resolution limitations. · Specialist reviewer: Cartography and search and rescue

N03 · USGS · What is a Topographic Map (current web edition)
Section: What is a Topographic Map > Contours and Relief · Conditions: Matching three-dimensional terrain shapes to two-dimensional contour lines
Support: Closed contour rings denote peaks or depressions (hachured), V-patterns point uphill in valleys/drainages and wide spacing marks gentle slopes while tight spacing marks cliffs or steep grades.
Limitations: Dense forest canopy or snow drifts can obscure subtle terrain features shown on the map. · Conflicting evidence: None; core topographic terrain-association evidence.
Editorial note: Supports ridge, valley, saddle and handrail terrain-interpretation cards. · Specialist reviewer: Navigation and cartography

N15 · NC State Extension · Using a Compass and Pacing (Reviewed/revised 2024-02-29)
Section: Pacing, final two paragraphs · Conditions: Measuring distance by pacing
Support: Pacing can be fairly accurate on level, open ground with practice. On slopes and in brushy or rocky areas its accuracy diminishes; the count is adjusted for the shortened pace and accuracy is maintained only by constant checking.
Limitations: Paced offsets are approximate; a matched count does not confirm position. · Conflicting evidence: None.
Editorial note: Added in W2C-R1 for RT01-08. · Specialist reviewer: Navigation and outdoor education
Primary source · checked 2026-11-28

X12 · US EPA · What is a Wetland (current web edition)
Section: What is a Wetland > Bogs, Fens, Swamps and Marshes · Conditions: Upper Midwest boreal bogs, marshes, beaver ponds and coastal wetlands
Support: Wetlands feature saturated hydric soils, floating vegetation mats (quaking bogs), hidden channels and fluctuating water tables that conceal deep muck, unstable footing and slow-draining floodwaters.
Limitations: Winter ice over bogs and wetlands is especially erratic due to decaying organic heat and seepage. · Conflicting evidence: Aligns with B014-P12 and NPS-WETLAND.
