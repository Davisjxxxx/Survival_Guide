Tie and verify a bowline for a non-slip fixed utility loop

Form, dress and safety-back a bowline loop that holds firm under steady tension yet unties cleanly afterward.

When it applies · Securing tarp ridgelines, pack haul loops and non-life-safety anchor loops on utility cordage.

Equipment · Pliable utility cordage practiced dry before field reliance.

Actions

  1. Form an overhand loop in the standing part, pass the working end up through the loop, around behind the standing part and back down through the same loop parallel to its entry.[K14, ED]
  2. Dress the knot cleanly by seating the collar snugly with a generous working tail, and add a stopper knot (such as a double overhand) on the tail—standard bowlines can shake loose under cyclic slack-and-tug loads in slick synthetic cord.[K14, ED]
  3. Inspect the finished knot before loading: the collar hugs the standing part, the small loop grips the two parallel strands, and the tail sits inside the loop with its stopper tied.[K14, ED]

Failure recognition · Tying a capsized slip loop by pulling the working end wrong, or leaving a tiny untied tail on slick nylon cord that works free in gusty wind.[K09, E16]

Verify · Check the collar shape, confirm the loop does not slip smaller when pulled and confirm the tail stopper knot is seated snugly.[K14, ED]

Stop or change course · Never rely on an unverified or un-backed bowline for critical tensioned loads; keep utility bowlines strictly separate from life-safety climbing harnesses.[K09]

E16 · NWS · Wind Safety (current web edition)
Section: Wind Safety > High Wind Hazards · Conditions: Forecast or developing high winds, squalls, or straight-line winds
Support: High winds topple trees, loft debris, and stress lightweight shelters beyond fabric and anchor limits; relocate out of timbered fall zones and into substantial shelter before gusts peak.
Limitations: Tents and tarps lack standardized wind-speed survival guarantees across soil types and pitch angles. · Conflicting evidence: None; avoids unvalidated mph ratings for improvised pitches.
Editorial note: Drives high-wind shelter inspection, guying limits, and abandonment thresholds. · Specialist reviewer: Weather safety and shelter equipment

K09 · USDA Forest Service · Handtools for Trail Work (0523-2810-MTDC) (agency handbook)
Section: Cordage, Anchors and Rigging Boundaries · Conditions: Non-life-safety camp lashing, tarps, haul lines and tie-downs
Support: Cordage strength drops significantly at knots, sharp bends, wet fibers, UV exposure and abrasion; inspect fibers before loading and never use unrated camp cord or improvised bindings for life-safety suspension or climbing.
Limitations: No universal breaking strength applies to natural plant fibers or weathered salvaged rope. · Conflicting evidence: Counters folklore claims equating improvised cordage to rated climbing rope.
Editorial note: Separates camp utility lashing/hauling from life-safety rigging. · Specialist reviewer: Rigging safety and materials mechanics

K14 · HQ Department of the Army · FM 3-97.61 Military Mountaineering, chapter 4 rope management and knots (approved for public release) (August 2002 edition; superseded by TC 3-97.61 (July 2012))
Section: Chapter 4 Section III, 4-12 Bowline: tying steps and checkpoints (pages 4-13 to 4-14) · Conditions: Tying a single fixed loop in the end of a rope
Support: The bowline ties a single fixed loop in the end of a rope. Checkpoints: the bight is locked into place by a loop; the short portion of the bight is inside the fixed loop; a minimum 4-inch pigtail remains after the overhand safety is tied.
Limitations: Written for trained users of mountaineering rope. Gives no rating for utility cord and does not make an improvised loop suitable for life support. Superseded 2002 edition; the 2012 TC 3-97.61 wording was not checked. · Conflicting evidence: None; the card sequence and inside-tail finish match these checkpoints.
