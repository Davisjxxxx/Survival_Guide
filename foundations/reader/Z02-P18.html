Z02-P18 · Shelter and housing · Adapted historical material

Orient shelter entrances relative to prevailing wind and storm shifts

Position low aerodynamic faces toward wind while protecting entrances from driven rain, snow drift and gusts.

When it applies · Pitching manufactured tents, tarps or emergency windbreaks in breezy, wet or winter conditions.

Equipment · Weather forecast showing wind shift direction, shelter guyline kit and natural terrain wind cues.

Actions

  1. Observe cloud motion, tree sway, snow drift tails and forecast frontal passages to identify both current wind direction and expected overnight wind shifts.[E15, E16, ED]
  2. Aim the lowest, narrowest or most reinforced side of the shelter into the expected wind and place the door on the leeward or crosswind side to limit blowing rain and drifting snow.[E15, E16, E20]
  3. Avoid pitching an open tarp mouth directly into oncoming wind—open fronts act like parachutes that tear tie-outs and strip away trapped calm air.[E16, E05]

Failure recognition · Facing a tent door into an approaching cold front because the sunset view faces west, flooding the vestibule and packing snow across the zipper.[E15, E16, E25]

Verify · Confirm the windward profile is low and guyed cleanly while the doorway opens into calmer leeward air with a clear exit.[E15, E16, ED]

Stop or change course · If shifting gusts push against an exposed broad wall and threaten pole or anchor failure, re-orient before dark or move to a more sheltered site.[E16, E05]

E05 · NWS · What To Do If You're Caught in a Winter Storm (current web edition)
Section: Outside and find shelter · Conditions: Cold, wet or windy winter conditions
Support: Block wind, stay dry and keep off cold ground. Keep exposure reduction separate from making a fire.
Limitations: Improvised windbreaks do not provide rated thermal protection or snow-load capacity. · Conflicting evidence: None; aligns with CDC-H.
Editorial note: Separates wind, ground, and moisture heat-loss pathways. · Specialist reviewer: Cold-weather shelter specialist

E15 · NWS · Cold Weather Safety (current web edition)
Section: Cold Weather Safety > Wind Chill and Dressing for Cold · Conditions: Cold, windy conditions where exposed skin cools rapidly
Support: Wind removes the thin warm air boundary layer around skin and permeable fabrics, sharply accelerating heat loss and frostbite onset; windproof outer shells and sheltered microclimates mitigate convective loss.
Limitations: Wind chill charts apply to exposed skin and do not change freezing point of water or equipment. · Conflicting evidence: None; clarifies physical mechanism of convective heat loss.
Editorial note: Supports wind-block geometry, shell layering, and wind-chill risk decisions. · Specialist reviewer: Cold-weather shelter and meteorology

E16 · NWS · Wind Safety (current web edition)
Section: Wind Safety > High Wind Hazards · Conditions: Forecast or developing high winds, squalls, or straight-line winds
Support: High winds topple trees, loft debris, and stress lightweight shelters beyond fabric and anchor limits; relocate out of timbered fall zones and into substantial shelter before gusts peak.
Limitations: Tents and tarps lack standardized wind-speed survival guarantees across soil types and pitch angles. · Conflicting evidence: None; avoids unvalidated mph ratings for improvised pitches.
Editorial note: Drives high-wind shelter inspection, guying limits, and abandonment thresholds. · Specialist reviewer: Weather safety and shelter equipment

Limitations: Manufacturer comfort ratings assume dry insulation, ground pad use, and adequate caloric intake. · Conflicting evidence: None; reinforces pre-trip inspection and dry-sleep separation.

E25 · FEMA / Ready.gov · Winter Weather (current web edition)
Section: Winter Weather > Stay Safe During Winter Storms · Conditions: Extreme cold, drifting snow, and power or travel disruption
Support: Prepare layered clothing, emergency shelter/heat alternatives, and vehicle winter kits; stay indoors during blizzards, protect against frostbite/hypothermia, and keep exhaust vents and exits clear of drifting snow.
