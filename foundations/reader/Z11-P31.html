Z11-P31 · Navigation, weather and rescue · Adapted historical material

Follow a compass bearing in forest using short visible leapfrog targets

Hold a straight intended course through trees and uneven terrain without staring down at the compass needle while walking.

When it applies · Short verified compass legs across woods, clearings or rolling terrain with safe visibility.

Equipment · Baseplate compass and partner (optional leapfrog spotter).

Actions

  1. Sight along the compass direction-of-travel arrow to pick an unmistakable intermediate visual target (a distinct tree trunk, boulder or stump) ahead on your line, then lower the compass and walk smoothly to that target watching your footing.[N08, E01, ED]
  2. At the intermediate target, sight back briefly toward your previous spot to confirm straightness, then pick the next visible target ahead along the same bearing—never hike with eyes locked on the needle.[N08, ED]
  3. With a partner in thick woods, send one person ahead within clear sight/voice range while the rear compass holder signals left/right alignment until centered on the bearing, then leapfrog forward.[N08, E11, ED]

Failure recognition · Walking while staring down at a bouncing compass needle, tripping on roots and unconsciously drifting sideways around brush piles.[N08, T01]

Verify · Confirm each intermediate visual target aligns closely with both your forward bearing and your backward check toward the last point.[N08, ED]

Stop or change course · When dense fog or brush collapses sighting distance to only a few paces and hazards surround you, halt compass travel and rely on verified handrails or stay put.[N07, N08]

N08 · NOAA NCEI · Magnetic Declination (current web edition)
Section: Magnetic Declination > Local Anomalies and Compass Interference · Conditions: Using magnetic baseplate compasses near metal, electronics or iron-rich rock
Support: True north, grid north and magnetic north differ by local declination; additionally, nearby phones, vehicles, knives, steel poles and localized magnetic rock (e.g., northern Minnesota iron formations) deflect compass needles erratically.
Limitations: Never assume a compass needle reads true magnetic field while held near gear, vehicles or iron ore. · Conflicting evidence: Aligns with B014-P13 and B014-P14.
