Waterborne Pathogen, HAB Cyanotoxin and Chemical Hazard Index

Cross-index biological vs chemical water hazards across backcountry (Z04), extreme environments (Z12), and household, well outages (Z13).

When it applies · Evaluating unfamiliar lakes, streams, floodwaters, snowmelt, and flooded private wells.

Equipment · Full offline reader (Z04, Z12, Z13), primary zone cards, and local emergency and agency references.

Actions

  1. Match microbial barriers to pathogen size: hollow-fiber microfilters remove bacteria and protozoa (Giardia, Cryptosporidium) but require boiling or chemical, UV viral steps where viruses exist, and freeze-cracked filters must be discarded (Z04-P37, Z04-P39).[A03, W15, W16]
  2. Preserve Cryptosporidium chlorine-resistance and cold-water chemical-disinfection caveats (Z04-P36) alongside elevation-adjusted rolling boil times (Z04-P34).[A03, W11, W14]
  3. Reject HAB cyanobacteria blooms, agricultural, mine, industrial runoff, brackish, seawater, and post-flood untested private wells (Z04-P14, Z12-P65, Z13-P41) because boiling and standard filters do not remove salts, metals, nitrates, or cyanotoxins.[A03, W04, W05, U11]

Failure recognition · Boiling cyanotoxin water or drinking seawater, nitrate well water worsens poisoning and dehydration.[W04, W05, U11]

Verify · Pathogen class, filter freeze status, altitude boiling, and chemical, HAB rejections are all checked together.[W04, W11, W15]

Stop or change course · Stop drinking any source with oil sheen, chemical smell, algal scum, flood sewage, or salt taste; switch to verified safe water.[W04, W05]

Section: What to DO; What Not to Do · Conditions: Suspected gas or pipeline leak
Support: Leave the affected area and call 911 and the provider from safety. Avoid spark sources and leave valve and restoration decisions to authorized responders.
Limitations: Do not transpose outdoor hazmat sheltering directions to a gas leak inside the dwelling. · Conflicting evidence: See A02 claim audit for boundaries.

W04 · CDC · Water Treatment Options When Hiking, Camping, or Traveling (current web edition)
Section: Water treatment methods > Filter and disinfect · Conditions: Untreated backcountry water; suitable devices and directions
Support: Filter capability varies. Filtering then disinfecting addresses gaps left by typical portable filters. Follow both manufacturers' directions.
Limitations: Effectiveness depends on pore rating, cartridge integrity, and manufacturer-verified test standards. · Conflicting evidence: None; consumer claims outside NSF/EPA standards are excluded.
Editorial note: Used to require multi-barrier pairing rather than relying on one filter alone. · Specialist reviewer: Drinking-water treatment specialist

W05 · CDC · How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency (2024-09-19)
Section: Disinfect; Using light · Conditions: Matched product, concentration, dose, contact time and source condition
Support: Chemical disinfection is less dependable against some parasites. UV needs clear water and a correctly used device.
Limitations: Cold temperature and organic turbidity reduce chemical efficacy and UV penetration. · Conflicting evidence: None; exact manufacturer label governs dose and wait time.
Editorial note: Prohibits improvised chemical dosing when labels are absent. · Specialist reviewer: Drinking-water treatment specialist

W11 · CDC · Water Treatment Options When Hiking, Camping, or Traveling (current web edition)
Section: Using light · Conditions: Portable UV disinfection device on a supported batch size of clear water
Support: Cloudiness interferes with UV. Use the device's stated volume, mixing and cycle instructions.
Limitations: Battery failure, cold battery voltage drop, or container UV opacity can silently defeat treatment. · Conflicting evidence: None; requires pre-clarification for turbid sources.
Editorial note: Requires verifying clarity, rated volume, and completed cycle indicator. · Specialist reviewer: Drinking-water treatment and equipment safety
Primary source · checked 2026-11-24
W14 · CDC · How to Create an Emergency Water Supply (current web edition)
Section: How Much Water to Store; Rotation · Conditions: Household emergency water reserve planning
Support: Plan at least 1 gallon per person per day for at least 3 days (more for heat, illness, pregnancy, or pets); rotate self-filled water every 6 months.
Limitations: Backcountry active travel demands vary sharply by exertion, temperature, and humidity. · Conflicting evidence: None; household baseline is cited only as a planning floor, not a physiological maximum.
Editorial note: Distinguishes household storage floors from active field hydration demands. · Specialist reviewer: Household preparedness and drinking-water safety
Primary source · checked 2026-11-24
W15 · US EPA · Learn About Harmful Algae, Cyanobacteria and Cyanotoxins (current web edition)
Section: Cyanotoxins and water treatment · Conditions: Surface water with discolored scum, mats, foul odor, or active HAB advisory
Support: Do not use a suspect bloom as a drinking-water source. Routine field microbial treatment is not a validated method of removing cyanotoxins.
Limitations: Cyanotoxins can persist in visually clear water after a bloom dissipates. · Conflicting evidence: Contradicts naive boil-everything advice; CDC/EPA HAB warnings explicitly prohibit boiling HAB water.
Editorial note: Establishes HABs as a chemical-toxin rejection trigger, not a microbial boil target. · Specialist reviewer: Environmental health and drinking-water safety
Primary source · checked 2026-11-24
W16 · U.S. EPA · Harmful Algal Blooms and Cyanotoxins FAQ (2016)
Section: Can I boil my water to make it safe to drink? · Conditions: Suspected cyanotoxins or applicable do-not-drink water advisory
Support: Boiling does not remove cyanotoxins and can increase their concentration. Follow the advisory and use an approved alternate source.
Limitations: Do not infer treatment capability or absence of toxin from visual appearance. · Conflicting evidence: See A02 claim audit for boundaries.
Editorial note: Supports regional Upper Midwest lake/pond source screening in summer and autumn. · Specialist reviewer: Environmental health and public health
