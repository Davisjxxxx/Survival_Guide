Z04-P15 · Water · New manuscript

Separate visual clarity from biological and chemical safety

Distinguish visible turbidity from invisible pathogens and dissolved chemical contaminants when evaluating water.

When it applies · Any untreated surface water, rain, snowmelt, spring or well source in backcountry or disaster settings.

Equipment · Source assessment checklist, validated microbial treatment and known-safe fallback supply.

Actions

  1. Treat clear appearance, cold temperature and fast flow as non-indicators of safety; bacteria, viruses, cysts and dissolved chemicals are invisible.[W09, W22, W24]
  2. Screen for chemical, fuel, heavy-metal, nitrate and algal-toxin hazards first because ordinary field treatments cannot remove them.[W01, W15, W22]
  3. Clarify cloudy water only as a preparatory step before applying an effective microbial barrier matched to expected pathogens.[W08, W04, W02]

Failure recognition · Drinking untreated clear mountain stream water or treating cloth-strained clear water as ready to drink.[W09, W08]

Verify · State separately how suspended solids, pathogens and chemical hazards were each ruled out or addressed before drinking.[W01, W08, W09, ED]

Stop or change course · If chemical safety cannot be reasonably established or microbial treatment is missing, reject the water and use an alternate supply.[W01, W09]

W01 · CDC · How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency (2024-09-19)
Section: Water with fuel or toxic chemicals in it · Conditions: Suspected fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive contamination
Support: Use a different safe supply. Standard boiling and microbial disinfection do not make this water safe.
Limitations: Does not list contaminant-specific lab remediation; applies to emergency consumer decisions only. · Conflicting evidence: None across CDC/EPA emergency guidance.
Editorial note: Used as upstream hard-stop gate prior to any field treatment card. · Specialist reviewer: Drinking-water safety and public health

W02 · CDC · How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency (2024-09-19)
Section: Boil > Steps for boiling water · Conditions: Microbiologically suspect water with acceptable chemical source; portable heat and safe vessel
Support: Clarify cloudy water first. Rolling boil for one minute; above 6,500 feet for three minutes. Cool and cover in sanitized containers.
Limitations: Does not remove dissolved metals, nitrates, fuel, or cyanotoxins. · Conflicting evidence: Older Track B B009 omitted the 6,500 ft threshold; CDC threshold governs.
Editorial note: Require explicit timer verification after rolling boil starts. · Specialist reviewer: Drinking-water safety and public health

W04 · CDC · Water Treatment Options When Hiking, Camping, or Traveling (current web edition)
Section: Water treatment methods > Filter and disinfect · Conditions: Untreated backcountry water; suitable devices and directions
Support: Filter capability varies. Filtering then disinfecting addresses gaps left by typical portable filters. Follow both manufacturers' directions.
Limitations: Effectiveness depends on pore rating, cartridge integrity, and manufacturer-verified test standards. · Conflicting evidence: None; consumer claims outside NSF/EPA standards are excluded.
Editorial note: Used to require multi-barrier pairing rather than relying on one filter alone. · Specialist reviewer: Drinking-water treatment specialist

W08 · CDC · How to Make Water Safe in an Emergency (2024-09-19)
Section: Boil > Steps for boiling water > cloudy water · Conditions: Cloudy water with no identified chemical hazard
Support: Settling or cloth clarification only prepares water for treatment. Do not count it as disinfection.
Limitations: Removes coarse suspended solids only; dissolved toxins and microbes remain. · Conflicting evidence: Contradicts folklore claims that sand/cloth straining yields potable water; CDC governs.
Editorial note: Always depicted upstream of microbial treatment with an explicit barrier line. · Specialist reviewer: Drinking-water safety

W09 · CDC · Water Treatment Options When Hiking, Camping, or Traveling (current web edition)
Section: Overview and water treatment methods · Conditions: Hiking or camping where no safe source is confirmed
Support: Even clear water can contain germs. Assess the supply and carry safe water or an appropriate treatment method.
Limitations: Visual clarity never detects microbes or dissolved chemical contaminants. · Conflicting evidence: None across CDC/NPS/EPA sources.

W15 · US EPA · Learn About Harmful Algae, Cyanobacteria and Cyanotoxins (current web edition)
Section: Cyanotoxins and water treatment · Conditions: Surface water with discolored scum, mats, foul odor, or active HAB advisory
Support: Do not use a suspect bloom as a drinking-water source. Routine field microbial treatment is not a validated method of removing cyanotoxins.
Limitations: Cyanotoxins can persist in visually clear water after a bloom dissipates. · Conflicting evidence: Contradicts naive boil-everything advice; CDC/EPA HAB warnings explicitly prohibit boiling HAB water.
Editorial note: Establishes HABs as a chemical-toxin rejection trigger, not a microbial boil target. · Specialist reviewer: Environmental health and drinking-water safety
Primary source · checked 2026-11-24
W22 · US EPA · National Primary Drinking Water Regulations (current web edition)
Section: National Primary Drinking Water Regulations · Conditions: Surface or groundwater near agriculture, industry, mines, roads, or old plumbing
Support: Regulated drinking-water hazards span inorganic chemicals (lead, arsenic, nitrate), organic solvents, pesticides, radionuclides, and microbes; microbial treatments do not remove dissolved inorganic or organic chemicals.
Limitations: Field observation cannot measure dissolved chemical concentration; laboratory testing is required. · Conflicting evidence: None; underpins strict chemical-rejection boundaries.
Editorial note: Explains why agricultural runoff, mine tailings, and road salt/fuel require source rejection. · Specialist reviewer: Environmental toxicology and drinking-water safety
Primary source · checked 2026-11-24
W24 · USGS · Turbidity and Water (current web edition)
Section: Turbidity and Water · optical definition and causes · Conditions: Cloudy, silt-laden, or storm-runoff streams and lakes
Support: Turbidity measures light scattering. Clay, silt and organic or biological material can contribute to turbidity.
Limitations: The USGS measurement is not evidence of potability, actual device clogging, or shielding magnitude. Follow validated treatment guidance. · Conflicting evidence: None; supports physical mechanism of pre-clarification.
Editorial note: Explains physical failure of filters and UV in turbid storm runoff. · Specialist reviewer: Hydrology and drinking-water treatment
